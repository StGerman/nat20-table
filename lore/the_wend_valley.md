# The Wend valley

A quiet farming valley at the far eastern end of a small kingdom whose lord rarely looks this far. Written for *The Silent Mill*; *Fair in Wend* and *The Way Back* use it too, and other packs may.

## Places

- **The river Wend** runs south out of the Hollin Hills, slow and brown, and turns every mill in the valley. Above the mouth of the Millbeck the mill weirs and the fords close it to boats; below it, the Saltmouth boatmen pole flat barges between the Millbeck mouth and the town. A barge goes down with the current in a day and comes back up only on a tow rope, with horses on the bank. A farmer with a wagon loads it once in the farmyard and drives it to the fair, which is cheaper than carting it to the river, loading it again and paying the boatmen.
- **Wendford** is a village of forty households where the old road crosses the river: a green with a well and an old oak, the reeve's house with its porch on the green, a shrine to Embra, sheep on the slopes. The market town is two days downriver by the river road.
- **Midwend** (Мидвенд) is a market village halfway down the river road: twelve miles below Wendford to the Millbeck bridge, fourteen more to Midwend, fourteen more to Saltmouth. Its green fills with carts on their way to the fair, and its inn, the Halfway, is where they stop for the night.
- **Pell's mill** stands a mile upriver at the end of a hedged lane: a stone mill with an undershot wheel and a sluice gate above it, a cobbled yard with a woodpile and an old kennel, a storeroom inside the door, and a cellar under the stones where the grain is kept dry. A back door lets out onto the millrace.
- **The Hollin Hills** close the valley to the north: heather, bog and old barrows. Beyond them is nobody's land; the hills are the valley's edge, not a border.
- **The Highsteads** are upland farms ten miles up the slopes from Wendford, under the hills: wool, honey and peat. Above the farms stand the summer huts on the high pasture, where the families take the flocks from late spring to the first frost.
- **Bald Hill** (Лысая гора) is the bare hill above the Highsteads, on the path to the Barrow Oak. Col and Dunn's farm stands on its shoulder. The valley called it Bald Hill long before anyone now living, and people once lit fires on its top on certain nights; nobody has lit them in living memory, and nobody says why any more.
- **The Barrow Oak** is a hollow oak on a barrow at the valley's northern edge. The hill folk keep away from it, except to push a copper into its roots when they want something they cannot ask a person for.
- **Saltmouth** is the fair town and the valley's port, where the Wend meets the sea, forty miles downriver from Wendford: two days with a wagon. It sells salt, iron and cloth, and people from every port pass through it. Its harbour is silting up: the old quay under the town walls stands on mud at low water, and ships now lie at the outer quay on Cork Island (остров Пробка), fifteen miles down Saltmouth Firth (Солтмутский залив) at its mouth, where the water in the Cork, the strait between the island and Dancers' Cape (мыс Плясунов), is still deep. The lord's gallows stands on Dancers' Cape over the strait, and every ship comes in under it. Within sight of it stands the Lord's Larder (Господская кладовая), the lord's prison: runaways caught on their way to the Free Host, debtors from the valley among them, men who sold salt outside the charter, pirates from the islands. Between its walls and the Free Host lie the firth, Sometimes Island, the old channel, the flats and the Dyke; between its walls and the gallows, a short walk. Lighters carry their cargo up to the town, half a day on the flood tide. Cork Island is a horseshoe round a small bay that opens to the sea. The quay, where the lighters load, is on its west arm, on the deep water of the Cork. The quarantine yard is on its east arm, with its anchorage under a yellow flag off the shore, where a ship from a far port waits out its days before anyone from it may go up the firth. The harbourmaster's tower (башня капитана порта) stands at the head of the bay, between the two, and the harbourmaster keeps both from it. The caravanserai (караван-сарай) stands on the north-west corner of Sometimes Island, across the old channel from the town walls: a house for anyone who pays a night, a square of walls round a yard with a well, stores and stables below, rooms above, one gate shut at dusk. Strangers from far ports and caravans off the old road lodge and trade there, a mile from the town and never inside it: at low water they walk across, at high water they are rowed.
- **The old channel** is an arm of the river that leaves Saltmouth eastward to the sea. The road from town runs six miles east along its near bank, past the lord's old salt pans, to the old ferry landing, where the channel can be waded at low tide. Across it stand an empty customs house and the salt flats: pans, mud and creeks, walked at low tide. Three miles further east is the Dyke (Вал), an old earth bank that runs inland from the sea along the far edge of the flats and ends where they do, and past it the plains.
- **Sometimes Island** (остров Иногда), south of the old channel between the firth and the Backdoor Bay (бухта Чёрный ход), is an island at high tide and a walk at low: low ground at the town end, a long back of rough pasture, and at its southern end Gawkers' Cape (мыс Зевак), which the crews in quarantine stare at for days across the water. At low water the smugglers' path comes off the salt flats where the old channel runs narrowest, by the island's east shore, and crosses the island's back to the caravanserai and the town.
- **The old road** runs west and east through Wendford. West it goes into the kingdom; east of the valley it leaves the kingdom across the Free Host's country. The caravans that use it come from far cities the valley rarely sees.

## The kingdom and its border

- The kingdom is small: a strip of coast and the valleys that run down to it. Its lord holds court in a harbour town about a day's sail west along the coast, which the valley has never seen.
- Past the Dyke, on the plains, live the **Free Host** (Вольница): free fighting men and their families under no lord. They keep a hard faith of their own, choose their captain in a circle, and bind themselves by oath, not by paper. They take in anyone who comes to them, clothe and arm them, and never hand them back: a runaway's debts do not follow him into their country. The Free Host are not the lord's subjects and owe him nothing.
- The border runs from the sea northward along the Dyke, then along the crest of the Snake (Змей), the long ridge that the hills send south between the valley and the plains, the way water parts: what drains to the Wend is the lord's, what drains east is the Free Host's. Its north end, the Head (Голова), rises into the hills, and the border stops at their foot; its south end, the Tail (Хвост), sinks into the low ground at the inland end of the Dyke. The old road east crosses the Snake by a pass, the Breach (Пролом), where the lord's soldiers say the Snake's back was broken. The Free Host say the Snake lies where it lay. Until last winter its last stretch, by the sea, was the old channel, and the salt flats across it were the Free Host's; the customs house on the far bank was theirs, where they asked who was crossing and took a ferry toll, never a duty on salt. The lord's border war was fought along the whole of it, mostly at the walls and breaches along the Snake, far from the flats; the pass took its name from the hardest of them.
- The peace was not drawn by either side. Both called in the **Wizards' Council** (Совет магов), a court and parliament of mages that sits in the trading cities over the sea, judges quarrels between lands and makes the laws for magic. The Council drew the new line on its map without setting foot on the flats: the flats went to the lord, and the border's last stretch moved from the old channel to the Dyke. The Free Host's captain swore to it before the Council. The Council's surveyors are setting stones along the Dyke, each cut with its mark; stones set by day are often gone by morning, and Saltmouth blames the Free Host. The ferry stopped, and the customs house stands empty.
- The battle-mages of the war fought under the Council's watch: what a mage may do in a war is the Council's law, not the lord's.

## Salt and Saltmouth's treasury

- Salt pays for Saltmouth. The old salt house holds the lord's charter for the old pans on the near bank, and the duty on every sack sold under it pays for the town's walls, the fair warden and the lighters to the outer quay. As the harbour silts and the outer quay costs more, the duty goes up: this is why salt is dear this year. Nobody up the valley knows it; they only see the price. Saltmouth's dealers know and keep it to themselves. No pack explains it on screen unless the pack says so.
- The charter does not name the new ground across the channel, so the pans there are worked outside it, and past the Dyke salt carries no duty at all. Salt that crosses the flats at low tide is a hole in the town's treasury, and the town has every reason to watch the flats.

## The sea

- From Saltmouth ships go west along the coast to the lord's harbour, south to small islands that belong to nobody and grow rich whenever a coast is closed to trade, and over the sea to the trading cities where the Wizards' Council sits. None of this is in `worlds/wend/map.json` yet.

## People and customs

- A **reeve** is a village's headman, chosen by the households and answerable to the lord's steward, who visits once a year. The reeve keeps the tally sticks, settles small quarrels and lends from the common chest.
- **Embra, the Hearth Mother**, is the valley's goddess of home fires, bread and mercy. Her sign is a round loaf scored with a cross. Her priests are expected to feed the hungry and to spare an enemy who yields.
- Most coin in the valley is silver pennies, with copper for small things; gold is merchants' money: it comes up the river in their purses and goes out as the pay of the Hired Blades they hire. A mill is the richest thing a village owns after its land, and a stopped one is felt in every kitchen within a week.

## The goblins of the hills

- Goblin bands are small, hungry and quarrelsome, each led by the strongest goblin that can keep it. They keep wolves for hunting and for war, and they speak Goblin among themselves; a few know enough Common to bargain.
- The **Nettle Tooth** band stains its teeth green with nettle juice. Down the valley it is known for stealing sheep, not for raiding farms.
- This autumn the bands have been moving south out of the hills. Hill folk say something new rules the barrows, something the goblins call **the Hollow King**, and that it takes tribute from every band. Nobody in Wendford knows more than the rumour.

## The Undersigned

The four pregenerated characters are a company of Hired Blades called **the Undersigned** (Нижеподписавшиеся; кого и о ком: Нижеподписавшихся, кому: Нижеподписавшимся, кем: Нижеподписавшимися).

- **The war.** They served together in a company of forty hired soldiers in the lord's border war. That company's name and banner were the lord's, and both went back to him; nobody uses the name now. They fought at the breaches along the Snake, far from the salt flats: they heard the border moved to the Dyke past the flats, but they have never been there and could not say where the line runs across the flats.
- **The sheet.** When the war ended last winter, the steward's clerk paid the company off with one sheet of forty lines, the names written in the autumn, at the hiring. Seven came to the barrel to sign. A mark against your name, and you took your purse. Old Holt (Холт), who cannot read, signed first, and three more after him. Ulf (Ульф), the youngest, was the first to read before he signed, and he read aloud what the four already stood under: "We, the undersigned, have been paid in full and make no claim, for ourselves or for those who have not signed below" («Мы, нижеподписавшиеся, получили сполна и претензий не имеем, ни за себя, ни за тех, кто ниже не подписался»). Thirty-three lines below were empty. Ulf put the pen down. The clerk said that unsigned, his share would stay where theirs did. Ulf was seventeen and expected at home, and he signed. Most of the thirty-three lie along the Snake: the dead do not come to a barrel. Their shares stayed in the lord's treasury.
- **The name.** Outside the gate a merchant looking for hands for his wagon asked whose men they were. Ulf said: the undersigned. He was not joking; the laughing came later.
- **The sign.** Seven small crosses stitched in a row on the sleeve, one for each mark on the sheet. Ulf stitched them for all seven that evening. A stranger takes them for a count of the living.
- **The custom.** The Undersigned sign nothing until it has been read aloud to them, all of it, to the last line. Merchants grumble that it takes long. The company waits.
- **The seven.** The four characters are four of the seven. Which of them signed before Ulf read is not fixed: a personality card may say. Holt, Ulf and one more took their own roads after that winter, and where they are now is not written yet. The four have travelled together since, taking guard work where they find it, and they reach Wendford on the morning the adventure begins.

### Reading and papers

- Every one of the four reads; nobody rolls to read a paper. Ilsa reads and writes for the company: when a paper is read aloud, she holds the sheet and follows the lines. Maren reads slowly and surely, and watches the reader, not the page. Torva can read, a finger under the line, and leaves it to Ilsa; she listens with her arms folded and remembers every word about money. Never say how the human's character reads: that is the player's.
- At a contract, whoever wrote the paper reads it aloud, all of it, and a Blade who reads follows the lines. *Fair in Wend* and *The Way Back* open after that: the paper has been read and marked. Say so once in the opening, in a clause.
- When a player asks what the paper says, say all of it, in the merchant's voice: who pays, the wage, what was paid ahead and where the rest is paid, as get_state's escort contract shows them; that a Blade who falls is paid for the days served; what becomes of the pay if the merchant dies, as the pack says. The paper holds nothing else.
- Never invent a clause, a penalty, a seal or a line in small letters; never hide a line because a roll failed; never tell the table the company missed something.
- Any other paper (a board, a tally, a letter): say what it says. Ask for a check (Investigation, the Blade who studies it as actor) only when the pack's text says what there is to find beyond the words. A failure never takes the words away: the other side sees the Blade looking for more.

The company tells the story as Holt told it, in his words. Tell it only when someone at the table asks how the company got its name or what the crosses are. A companion may begin it ("Holt told it like this"); never put it in the mouth of the human's character, and never add a moral:

> Я подписал первым. Читать я не умею, а считать умею.
>
> Война кончилась зимой. Писарь лорда положил на бочку лист в сорок строк. Имена он вписал ещё осенью, когда нас нанимали, все сорок. Нас к бочке подошло семеро.
>
> Порядок был простой: ставишь знак против своего имени, получаешь кошель. Я поставил. За мной ещё трое. Пятым стоял Ульф, самый младший. Он взялся читать и прочёл вслух то, под чем уже стояли наши четыре знака: «Мы, нижеподписавшиеся, получили сполна и претензий не имеем, ни за себя, ни за тех, кто ниже не подписался».
>
> Тридцать три строки ниже были пустые.
>
> Ульф положил перо. Писарь сказал: не подпишешь, твоя доля останется там же, где их. Ульф помолчал и подписал. Ему было семнадцать, и дома ждали.
>
> Знамя и имя были лордовы, мы сдали и то и другое. За воротами нас окликнул торговец, искал людей к фургону: чьи будете? Ульф ответил: нижеподписавшиеся. Он не шутил. Смеяться мы начали потом, не сразу.
>
> Вечером Ульф вышил каждому на рукав семь крестиков в ряд. Мой первый.
>
> С тех пор мы ничего не подписываем, пока нам не прочтут вслух. Всё, до последней строки. Торговцы злятся, что долго. Мы ждём.
